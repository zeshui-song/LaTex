The overarching goal of solid mechanics is to achieve satisfactory mechanical performance by bringing together loading, stress, strain, and material properties. \textit{This goal hasn't changed since Mechanics of Materials (ESC-201).} What is different in ME-300 is that we will be able to analyze more general and complex cases of stress and strain instead of simplified cases.

Regardless of the approach or model, given a body of a particular geometry, material, loading, and constraints, we seek to determine its mechanical response and evaluate whether it satisfies the design requirements.

\subsection*{Connecting Statics to Mechanics of Materials to Elasticity}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Statics: Forces acting on a body}\\
    In statics, bodies are typically treated as rigid. We are mostly concerned with the equilibrium of forces and moments in determining external reactions and internal forces.
    \[
\sum\vec F=0,\qquad \sum\vec M=0
\]
    However, we don't consider how the body deforms under the applied loads or how internal forces are distributed within the material.
    \item \textbf{Mechanics of Materials: Simplified models of deformable bodies}\\
    In MechMat, we took the ideas from statics and combined them with Materials Science to introduce stress, strain, and deformation. \hl{By making simplifying assumptions about geometry, loading and deformation, we obtain relatively simple analytical models.}\newpage

    \item \textbf{Theory of Elasticity: Generalized models of deformable bodies}\\
    Rather than applying the simplified models from MechMat, the theory of elasticity establishes the general equations that stress, strain, and displacement fields must satisfy.\\\\
    For example, instead of only calculating the bending stress at a particular cross-section, we can investigate an entire stress field:
\[
\sigma_x(x,y,z),\quad \sigma_y(x,y,z),\quad
\tau_{xy}(x,y,z),\quad \ldots
\]  
This allows us to investigate more complicated geometries, loading conditions and localized mechanical behavior.\\\\
Note that the MechMat models can be thought of as special, simplified cases of the general elasticity models!

\subsection*{Stress at a point vs stress on a plane}
In MechMat, we usually calculate normal and shear stresses on a particular cross-section of a body. However, at any given point within a body, we can consider an infinite number of planes passing through that point, each with its own unit normal vector describing its orientation.

The normal and shear stresses acting on each of these planes may be different, even though they describe the same physical state of stress at that point. In other words, changing the orientation of the plane changes the stresses acting on that plane without changing the underlying physical state of stress.

This method of describing stresses is useful in cases where there exists a joint (e.g. glued joint, welded joint, etc.) that is not aligned with the loading direction of a structural member. By using stress transformations, we can determine the normal and shear stresses acting on the plane containing the joint and better predict failure.
\img[]{stress in a plane.png}
Instead of independently calculating the stresses on infinitely many possible planes, we describe the local state of stress using a stress tensor. Then, on any given plane defined by its unit normal vector $\hat n$, we can calculate the force per unit area acting on that plane, called the traction vector $\vec p$. 
\subsection*{Traction Vector}
The traction vector \(\vec p\) describes the force per unit area acting on an infinitesimal surface with outward unit normal vector \(\hat n\).
\[
\boxed{\vec p=\lim_{\Delta A\to0}\frac{\Delta\vec F}{\Delta A}}
\]
The traction vector and unit normal vector are expressed in Cartesian coordinates as
\[
\vec p=p_x\hat i+p_y\hat j+p_z\hat k
\]
\[
\hat n=l\hat i+m\hat j+n\hat k
\]
where \(l,m,n\) are the \textbf{direction cosines}:
\[
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
l&=\cos\alpha=\hat n\cdot\hat i\\
m&=\cos\beta=\hat n\cdot\hat j\\
n&=\cos\gamma=\hat n\cdot\hat k
\end{aligned}}
\]
Here, \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\) are the angles between \(\hat n\) and the \(x,y,z\) axes, respectively.
\img[0.4]{direction cosine.png}
Since \(\hat n\) is a unit vector,
\[
l^2+m^2+n^2=1
\]
\textbf{Derivation of traction components}\\
Consider an infinitesimal tetrahedron with three faces oriented in the \(-x,-y,-z\) directions and a fourth face with outward unit normal \(\hat n\).
\img[0.5]{traction components.png}
From static equilibrium in the \(x\)-direction:
\[
\sum F_x=0\quad\implies\quad
p_x\Delta A-\sigma_xl\Delta A
-\tau_{yx}m\Delta A-\tau_{zx}n\Delta A=0
\]
\[
\boxed{p_x=\sigma_xl+\tau_{yx}m+\tau_{zx}n}
\]
Similarly, applying equilibrium in the \(y\) and \(z\) directions:
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
p_y&=\tau_{xy}l+\sigma_ym+\tau_{zy}n\\
p_z&=\tau_{xz}l+\tau_{yz}m+\sigma_zn
\end{aligned}}
\]
Recall that the first stress subscript denotes the face orientation, while the second denotes the direction in which the stress acts.
\img[0.4]{3D state of stress.png}
\textbf{Matrix representation}\\
Define the traction and normal vectors:
\[
\vec p=
\begin{bmatrix}
p_x\\p_y\\p_z
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\hat n=
\begin{bmatrix}
l\\m\\n
\end{bmatrix}
\]
Then, define the \textbf{stress tensor}:
\[
\boxed{
[\tau]\equiv
\begin{bmatrix}
\sigma_x&\tau_{xy}&\tau_{xz}\\
\tau_{yx}&\sigma_y&\tau_{yz}\\
\tau_{zx}&\tau_{zy}&\sigma_z
\end{bmatrix}
}
\]
The traction component equations can then be expressed as a matrix-vector product:
\[
\begin{bmatrix}
p_x\\p_y\\p_z
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\sigma_x&\tau_{yx}&\tau_{zx}\\
\tau_{xy}&\sigma_y&\tau_{zy}\\
\tau_{xz}&\tau_{yz}&\sigma_z
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
l\\m\\n
\end{bmatrix}
\]
Thus,
\[
\vec p=[\tau]^{tr}\cdot\hat n
\]
However, because the stress tensor is symmetric due to shear stress symmetry, $[\tau]^{tr}=[\tau]$. We can also write
\[
\boxed{\vec p=[\tau]\cdot\hat n}
\]
This is \textbf{Cauchy's traction equation}.
\newpage
\textbf{Normal and shear components of traction}\\
The traction vector can be decomposed into its normal and tangential components relative to the plane.
\img[0.5]{traction normal shear.png}
Normal stress: Projection of the traction vector onto the unit normal vector.
\[
\boxed{\sigma=\vec p\cdot\hat n}
\]
Substituting Cauchy's traction equation:
\[
\boxed{\sigma=\hat n^{tr}[\tau]\hat n}
\]
Shear stress vector: Remaining component of the traction vector after removing its normal component.
\[
\boxed{\vec \tau=\vec p-\sigma\hat n}
\]
Shear stress magnitude:
\[
\boxed{\tau=\sqrt{p_x^2+p_y^2+p_z^2-\sigma^2}}
\]
\textbf{Summary}\\
The stress tensor $[\tau]$ describes the complete state of stress at a point within a material. The traction vector $\vec p$ describes the force per unit area acting on a specific plane that passes through that point with outward normal vector $\hat n$. 
\section*{\fbox{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{1.2 Stress Transformations, Principal Stresses, and Stress Invariants}}}
\vspace{-0.5em}
{\small\textit{Ugural and Fenster 1.9--1.15}}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{1.2 Stress Transformations, Principal Stresses, and Stress Invariants}\\
Recall that the stress tensor describes the complete state of stress at a point within a material:
\[
[\tau]=
\begin{bmatrix}
\sigma_x&\tau_{xy}&\tau_{xz}\\
\tau_{yx}&\sigma_y&\tau_{yz}\\
\tau_{zx}&\tau_{zy}&\sigma_z
\end{bmatrix}
\]
However, the components of the stress tensor depend on the orientation of the coordinate system.

Stress transformations will allow us to determine the stress tensor components in a new coordinate system that is rotated relative to the original coordinate system, expressing the same physical state of stress relative to a different coordinate system.

This is useful in cases where a material's natural directions of strength are not aligned with the loading direction (such as wood grain or composite fibers), or in cases where failure is expected to occur along planes that are not aligned with the coordinate axes (such as joints, welds, or cracks). Furthermore, under combined loading (e.g. tension and torsion), the principal directions corresponding to the maximum and minimum normal stresses may not align with the original loading directions. Stress transformations allow us to determine the magnitudes and orientations of these principal stresses to better predict potential modes of failure.
\subsection*{3D Stress Transformations}
\textbf{Direction Cosine Matrix}\\
Let \(x,y,z\) denote the original coordinate system and \(x',y',z'\) denote the rotated coordinate system.

The corresponding unit vectors are
\[
(\hat \imath,\hat \jmath,\hat k),\qquad
(\hat \imath',\hat \jmath',\hat k')
\]
\img[0.35]{stress transformations.png}
Express the rotated unit vectors in terms of the original basis vectors:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\hat{\imath}'&=l_1\hat{\imath}+m_1\hat{\jmath}+n_1\hat{k}\\
\hat{\jmath}'&=l_2\hat{\imath}+m_2\hat{\jmath}+n_2\hat{k}\\
\hat{k}'&=l_3\hat{\imath}+m_3\hat{\jmath}+n_3\hat{k}
\end{aligned}
\]
The nine direction cosines are organized into the transformation matrix \([L]\):
\[
\boxed{
[L]=
\begin{bmatrix}
l_1&m_1&n_1\\
l_2&m_2&n_2\\
l_3&m_3&n_3
\end{bmatrix}
}
\]
Where each direction cosine is defined as the dot product of the rotated unit vector with the original unit vector:
{
\setlength{\arraycolsep}{30pt}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\[
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
 & \hat{\imath} & \hat{\jmath} & \hat{k}\\
\hline
\hat{\imath}' & l_1 & m_1 & n_1\\
\hat{\jmath}' & l_2 & m_2 & n_2\\
\hat{k}'      & l_3 & m_3 & n_3
\end{array}
\]
}
\begin{notebox}[]
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Dot products are commutative: $\vec a \cdot \vec b = \vec b \cdot \vec a$.
        \item But matrix multiplication is not commutative: $[A][B]\neq[B][A]$ in general.
        \item  Matrix multiplication can be thought of a series of dot products between the rows of the first matrix and the columns of the second matrix. \\\\
        For example:
        \[
\begin{bmatrix}
a & b\\
c & d
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
e & f\\
g & h
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
ae+bg & af+bh\\
ce+dg & cf+dh
\end{bmatrix}
\]
    \end{itemize}
\end{notebox}
Because both coordinate systems are orthonormal, it has the properties:
\[
\boxed{[L][L]^{tr}=
\begin{bmatrix}
\hat{\imath}'\cdot\hat{\imath}'&
\hat{\imath}'\cdot\hat{\jmath}'&
\hat{\imath}'\cdot\hat{k}'\\
\hat{\jmath}'\cdot\hat{\imath}'&
\hat{\jmath}'\cdot\hat{\jmath}'&
\hat{\jmath}'\cdot\hat{k}'\\
\hat{k}'\cdot\hat{\imath}'&
\hat{k}'\cdot\hat{\jmath}'&
\hat{k}'\cdot\hat{k}'
\end{bmatrix}
=[I]}
\]
\textit{After performing the matrix multiplication in terms of the direction cosines $l_1, m_1, n_1, \ldots$, recognize each resulting entry as the dot product of two rotated basis vectors.}
\[
\boxed{[L]^{-1}=[L]^{tr}}
\]
\textbf{Derivation of the stress transformation equation}\\
\underline{Step 1:} Calculate the traction vectors on the rotated coordinate planes

Recall Cauchy's traction equation from the previous section:
\[
\vec p=[\tau]\hat n
\]
For the three rotated coordinate planes, the outward unit normals are
$\hat{\imath}', \hat{\jmath}', \hat{k}'$.

Therefore,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\vec p_1&=[\tau]\hat{\imath}'\\
\vec p_2&=[\tau]\hat{\jmath}'\\
\vec p_3&=[\tau]\hat{k}'
\end{aligned}
\]
Combining these three traction vectors as columns of a matrix:
\[
\begin{bmatrix}
\vec p_1&\vec p_2&\vec p_3
\end{bmatrix}
=[\tau]
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
l_1&l_2&l_3\\
m_1&m_2&m_3\\
n_1&n_2&n_3
\end{bmatrix}
}_{[L]^{tr}}
=[\tau][L]^{tr}
\]
Now \(\vec p_1,\vec p_2,\vec p_3\) are the traction vectors acting on the planes with outward unit normals \(\hat{\imath}',\hat{\jmath}',\hat{k}'\), respectively. However, their \hl{components are still expressed relative to the original coordinate system.}

\underline{Step 2:} Project the traction vectors onto the rotated coordinate directions.

To obtain the stress components relative to the rotated coordinate system, take the dot product of each traction vector with the rotated unit vectors.

For example, consider for the plane normal to \(\hat{\imath}'\):
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_{x'}&=\vec p_1\cdot\hat{\imath}'\\
\tau_{x'y'}&=\vec p_1\cdot\hat{\jmath}'\\
\tau_{x'z'}&=\vec p_1\cdot\hat{k}'
\end{aligned}
\]

Repeating this for all three traction vectors and organizing the resulting dot products in matrix form:
\[\begin{aligned}
    \begin{bmatrix}
\hat{\imath}'\cdot\vec p_1&
\hat{\imath}'\cdot\vec p_2&
\hat{\imath}'\cdot\vec p_3\\
\hat{\jmath}'\cdot\vec p_1&
\hat{\jmath}'\cdot\vec p_2&
\hat{\jmath}'\cdot\vec p_3\\
\hat{k}'\cdot\vec p_1&
\hat{k}'\cdot\vec p_2&
\hat{k}'\cdot\vec p_3
\end{bmatrix}
=& \begin{bmatrix}
\sigma_{x'} & \tau_{y'x'} & \tau_{z'x'}\\
\tau_{x'y'} & \sigma_{y'} & \tau_{z'y'}\\
\tau_{x'z'} & \tau_{y'z'} & \sigma_{z'}
\end{bmatrix}\\
=&
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
l_1&m_1&n_1\\
l_2&m_2&n_2\\
l_3&m_3&n_3
\end{bmatrix}
}_{[L]}
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
\vec p_1&\vec p_2&\vec p_3
\end{bmatrix}
}_{[\tau][L]^{tr}}\\
=& [\tau']^{tr}
\end{aligned}
\]
Like before, since the stress tensor is symmetric, \([\tau']^{tr}=[\tau']\).

Therefore, the general three-dimensional stress transformation equation is
\[
\boxed{[\tau']=[L][\tau][L]^{tr}}
\]

Inverse stress transformation
Since \([L]^{-1}=[L]^{tr}\),
\[
\boxed{[\tau]=[L]^{tr}[\tau'][L]}
\]
\textit{Intuition:} We have to transform the stress tensor twice using the direction cosine matrix \([L]\). This makes sense since each stress component (e.g. $\tau_{xy}$) is defined in terms of TWO coordinate directions (one for the plane orientation and one for the stress direction)! 

Therefore, we need to transform both:
\[
\begin{gathered}
[\tau]\\
\text{Original planes, original component directions}\\[4pt]
\downarrow\\[4pt]
\text{Right-multiply by } [L]^{tr}\\
[\tau][L]^{tr}\\
\text{Rotated planes, original component directions}\\[4pt]
\downarrow\\[4pt]
\text{Left-multiply by } [L]\\
[L][\tau][L]^{tr}=[\tau']\\
\text{Rotated planes, rotated component directions}
\end{gathered}
\]
\begin{notebox}[]
    Since matrix multiplication is not commutative, the order of multiplication matters. We must multiply both sides of a matrix equation \textit{from the same side} to maintain equality.\\\\
    For example:
    \[
[A][B]=[C]
\]
Right-multiply both sides by $[B]^{-1}$:
\[
[A][B][B]^{-1}
=[C][B]^{-1}
\]
Then left-multiply both sides by $[A]^{-1}$:
\[[A]^{-1}[A]
=[A]^{-1}[C][B]^{-1}
\]
We get:
\[
[I]=[A]^{-1}[C][B]^{-1}
\]
\textit{The order of the left/right multiplication doesn't matter, so long as it is done consistently.}
\end{notebox}
\subsection*{Two-dimensional stress transformations}
The 2D stress transformations using Mohr's circle from MechMat are a special case of the general 3D stress transformations. By rotating the coordinate axes about a principal direction (an axis normal to a shear-free plane), we only need to consider the transformation of stresses in the remaining two coordinate directions.
\newpage
\textbf{Mohr's Circle}\\
Consider an in-plane rotation of the $x$-$y$ coordinate axes for a 2D state of stress in which the $z$-direction is a principal direction:
\img[0.5]{in plane rotation.png}
We can use the Mohr's circle construction to determine the normal and shear stresses acting on the rotated plane.
\img[0.8]{Mohr's circle.png}
\textit{Note: the physical state of stress is unchanged, we are simply determining the stress components acting on the rotated faces and expressing them in the rotated coordinate system.}
\newpage
\underline{Mohr's circle interpretation:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Plot points \(X\) and \(Y\) corresponding to the original, untransformed stress components:
\[
X=(\sigma_x,-\tau_{xy}),\qquad
Y=(\sigma_y,\tau_{xy})
\]
\item Calculate the average normal stress and difference in normal stresses:
\[
\sigma_{\mathrm{ave}}=\frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2},
\qquad \Delta\sigma=\sigma_x-\sigma_y
\]
\item Determine the center and radius of Mohr's circle:
\[
C=(\sigma_{\mathrm{ave}},0),\qquad
R=\sqrt{\left(\frac{\Delta\sigma}{2}\right)^2+\tau_{xy}^2}
\]
\item Construct Mohr's circle centered at \(C\) and passing through \(X\) and \(Y\).\\\\
\hl{All possible combinations of normal and shear stress obtained by rotating the coordinate axes about the $z$-axis are represented by points on Mohr's circle.}
\item \hl{A physical counterclockwise rotation of the coordinate axes by \mbox{$\theta$} corresponds to a counterclockwise rotation by \mbox{$2\theta$} on Mohr's circle:}
\img[0.7]{rotation on mohr's.png}
\end{enumerate}
\newpage
\textit{Summary of Mohr's Circle eqns:}\\
Define:
\[
\Delta\sigma=\sigma_x-\sigma_y,\qquad
\sigma_{ave}=\frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2},\qquad R=\sqrt{\left(\frac{\Delta\sigma}{2}\right)^2+\tau_{xy}^2}
\]
Transformed stresses:
\[
\boxed{
\sigma_{x'}=\sigma_{ave}
+\frac{\Delta\sigma}{2}\cos2\theta
+\tau_{xy}\sin2\theta
}
\]
\[
\boxed{
\sigma_{y'}=\sigma_{ave}
-\frac{\Delta\sigma}{2}\cos2\theta
-\tau_{xy}\sin2\theta
}
\]
\[
\boxed{
\tau_{x'y'}=\tau_{xy}\cos2\theta
-\frac{\Delta\sigma}{2}\sin2\theta
}
\]
\subsection*{2D Principal Stresses and Principal Directions}
A \textbf{principal plane} is a plane on which the traction vector is entirely normal to the plane, \hl{such that no shear stress exists.} The normal stress acting on that plane is called a 
\textbf{principal stress}, and its normal direction is called a 
\textbf{principal direction}.

From Mohr's circle, it can be seen that there are two principal 
directions, separated by $90^\circ$, corresponding to the maximum 
and minimum in-plane normal stresses (where $\tau_{x'y'}=0$):
\[
\boxed{\sigma_{\max}=\sigma_{\mathrm{ave}}+R,\quad
\sigma_{\min}=\sigma_{\mathrm{ave}}-R}
\]

In terms of the original stress components (substituting the 
equations for $\sigma_{\mathrm{ave}}$ and $R$):
\[
\boxed{
\sigma_{a,b}=
\frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2}
\pm\sqrt{
\left(\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\right)^2
+\tau_{xy}^2
}}
\]
\textit{Note: we are using $a,b$ to denote the principal stresses 
for now.}

The orientations of the principal directions can be determined from 
Mohr's circle:
\[
\boxed{\tan 2\theta_p=\frac{2\tau_{xy}}{\Delta\sigma}}
\]
where the two principal directions are separated by $90^\circ$:
\[
\theta_{p2}=\theta_{p1}+90^\circ
\]

\textbf{Maximum and minimum in-plane shear stresses}\\
From Mohr's circle, the maximum and minimum in-plane shear stresses 
occur where \\
$\sigma_{x'}=\sigma_{y'}=\sigma_{\mathrm{ave}}$:
\[
\boxed{\tau_{\max}=R,\quad\tau_{\min}=-R}
\]

In terms of the original stress components:
\[
\tau_{\max}=
\sqrt{
\left(\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\right)^2
+\tau_{xy}^2
}
\]

The orientations corresponding to the extreme shear stresses are:
\[
\boxed{\tan 2\theta_s=-\frac{\Delta\sigma}{2\tau_{xy}}}
\]
\img[0.45]{mohr's circle summary.png}
\textit{Where $\theta_p$ and $\theta_s$ are the angles corresponding to the principal directions and extreme shear stresses, respectively. Also note that the max/min in plane stresses are not necessarily the max/min stresses in 3D.}

\subsection*{Principal stresses in three dimensions}
The three-dimensional problem is a generalization of the two-dimensional principal stress problem. Recall Cauchy's traction equation:
\[
\vec p=[\tau]\hat n
\]
On a principal plane, the shear traction is zero by definition. 
\img[0.5]{3d principal.png}
Therefore,
\[
\vec p=\sigma_p\hat n
\]
Equating these expressions:
\[
[\tau]\hat n=\sigma_p\hat n
\]
Introducing the identity matrix \([I]\):
\[
\boxed{([\tau]-\sigma_p[I])\hat n=\vec 0}
\]
Expressing this explicitly:
\[
\begin{bmatrix}
\sigma_x-\sigma_p&\tau_{xy}&\tau_{xz}\\
\tau_{xy}&\sigma_y-\sigma_p&\tau_{yz}\\
\tau_{xz}&\tau_{yz}&\sigma_z-\sigma_p
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
l\\m\\n
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\0\\0
\end{bmatrix}
\]
If $([\tau]-\sigma_p[I])$ were invertible, multiplying both sides by its inverse would result in the trivial solution $\hat n=\vec 0$. However, since $\hat n$ is a unit vector, it cannot be zero. Therefore, we require a nontrivial solution, which occurs when the determinant of the matrix is zero (matrix is singular and not invertible):
\[
\boxed{\det([\tau]-\sigma_p[I])=0}
\]
This is the \textbf{characteristic equation} of the stress tensor.

This characteristic equation is an \textit{eigenvalue problem} where:
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabular}{l l l}
\hline
\textbf{Mathematical Quantity} & \textbf{Physical Interpretation} & \textbf{Notation} \\
\hline
Eigenvalues of $[\tau]$ & Principal stresses & $\sigma_p$ \\
Eigenvectors of $[\tau]$ & Associated principal axes (orientations) & $\hat{n}$ \\
Eigenvalue equation & Principal stress and orientation condition
& $([\tau]-\sigma_p[I])\cdot\hat{n}=\vec{0}$ \\
Characteristic equation & Condition for nontrivial solutions
& $\det([\tau]-\sigma_p[I])=0$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\textit{Note: The principal directions (eigenvectors) are obtained by substituting each principal stress $\sigma_p$ into 
$([\tau]-\sigma_p[I])\hat{n}=\vec{0}$ and solving for 
$\hat n = l\hat{\imath} + m\hat{\jmath} + n\hat{k}$ subject to $l^2+m^2+n^2=1$.}

For the three resulting principal stresses, we can denote them as $\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3$ such that
\[\sigma_1\geq\sigma_2\geq\sigma_3\]
Also note that in principal coordinates, the stress tensor is diagonal:
\[
[\tau']=[L][\tau][L]^{tr}
=\begin{bmatrix}
\sigma_1&0&0\\
0&\sigma_2&0\\
0&0&\sigma_3
\end{bmatrix}
\]
\textit{So if we choose the right principal direction cosine matrix $[L]$, we can diagonalize the stress tensor.}
\begin{notebox}[Stress Invariants]
    The individual components of the stress tensor change when we rotate the coordinate system. However, the principal stresses do not change because the underlying physical state of stress remains unchanged.\\\\
Expanding the characteristic equation,
\[
\det([\tau]-\sigma_p[I])=0
\]
gives
\[
\sigma_p^3-I_1\sigma_p^2+I_2\sigma_p-I_3=0
\]
where \(I_1,I_2,I_3\) are the three stress invariants.\\\\
These invariants give a good coordinate-independent sanity check for stress transformation calculations.
\[
\boxed{I_1=\sigma_x+\sigma_y+\sigma_z
=\sigma_{x'}+\sigma_{y'}+\sigma_{z'}
=\sigma_1+\sigma_2+\sigma_3}
\]
For two-dimensional transformations:
\[
\boxed{\sigma_x+\sigma_y=\sigma_{x'}+\sigma_{y'}}
\]
\textit{We already implicitly used this relationship when we
defined the center of the 2D Mohr's circle as the average
normal stress, which remains constant under coordinate rotation.}
\end{notebox}

\subsection*{Mohr's circles for three-dimensional stress}
Let $a,b,c$ denote three mutually perpendicular principal axes with corresponding principal stresses $\sigma_a,\sigma_b,\sigma_c$, without imposing any numerical ordering yet. In these principal coordinates, the transformed stress tensor is diagonal:
\[
[\tau']=[L][\tau][L]^{tr}=
\begin{bmatrix}
\sigma_a&0&0\\
0&\sigma_b&0\\
0&0&\sigma_c
\end{bmatrix}
\]
The plane normal is
\[
\hat n=l\hat a+m\hat b+n\hat c
\]
From the traction equation:
\[\begin{aligned}
\sigma=\vec p\cdot\hat n &= (\sigma_a l)l+(\sigma_b m)m+(\sigma_c n)n\\
&= \sigma_al^2+\sigma_bm^2+\sigma_cn^2
\end{aligned}
\]
\[
\tau=\sqrt{p_x^2+p_y^2+p_z^2-\sigma^2}
\quad\implies\quad 
\tau^2=(\sigma_a l)^2+(\sigma_b m)^2+(\sigma_c n)^2-\sigma^2
\]
\[
\tau^2=\sigma_a^2l^2+\sigma_b^2m^2+\sigma_c^2n^2-\sigma^2
\]
Consider a rotation about the principal axis $a$ (normal to the $bc$ plane). Then $l=0$, substituting and simplifying gives the equation of a circle in the $\sigma$-$\tau$ plane:
\[
\boxed{
\left(\sigma-\frac{\sigma_b+\sigma_c}{2}\right)^2
+\tau^2
=\left(\frac{\sigma_b-\sigma_c}{2}\right)^2
}
\]
Where the center is at $\sigma_{\mathrm{ave}}=(\sigma_b+\sigma_c)/2$ and the radius is $R=(\sigma_b-\sigma_c)/2$. This matches the 2D Mohr's circle construction.

We can therefore generalize:
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabular}{l l l}
\hline
\textbf{Axis of rotation} & \textbf{Constraint} & \textbf{Principal stresses defining circle} \\
\hline
$\hat{a}$ & $l=0$ & $\sigma_b,\sigma_c$ \\
$\hat{b}$ & $m=0$ & $\sigma_a,\sigma_c$ \\
$\hat{c}$ & $n=0$ & $\sigma_a,\sigma_b$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Each Mohr's circle represents rotation about the principal axis \hl{whose corresponding principal stress is not used to define that circle.} This holds regardless of the numerical ordering.

\textbf{Constructing the 3D Mohr's Circle}\\
Only after establishing the principal stresses and their corresponding directions do we assign the numerical subscripts, ordering $\sigma_a,\sigma_b,\sigma_c$ from largest to smallest:
\[
\sigma_1\geq\sigma_2\geq\sigma_3
\]
Plotting the three Mohr's circles in the $\sigma$-$\tau$ plane, we have:
\img[0.6]{3d mohrs.png}
\hl{Where the allowable stress states lie within the shaded region bounded by the three Mohr's circles.}

The largest circle is defined by $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_3$, representing rotation about the principal axis corresponding to $\sigma_2$. Similarly, each of the other two circles represents rotation about the principal axis associated with the remaining principal stress.

\textbf{Absolute maximum shear and normal stresses}\\
Like the 2D Mohr's circle, the absolute maximum shear stress corresponds to the radius of the largest Mohr's circle:
\[
\boxed{\tau_{\max}=\frac{\sigma_1-\sigma_3}{2}}
\]

Likewise, the absolute maximum and minimum normal stresses correspond to the limits of the largest Mohr's circle:
Maximum and minimum normal stresses:
\[
\boxed{\sigma_{\max}=\sigma_1,\qquad
\sigma_{\min}=\sigma_3}
\]
\begin{notebox}[Problem-Solving Approach: 3D Mohr's Circles]
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Before performing stress transformations, examine the geometry, loading conditions, boundary conditions, and resulting stress element to identify any existing principal directions.

    \item If one principal direction is already known, the remaining two principal stresses and directions can be determined through a two-dimensional stress transformation about that axis.

    \item After determining all three principal stresses and their corresponding directions, order the stresses such that
    \[
    \sigma_1\geq\sigma_2\geq\sigma_3
    \]
    and construct the 3D Mohr's circles accordingly.
\end{enumerate}

\hl{Many seemingly three-dimensional stress analysis problems can be reduced to familiar two-dimensional stress transformation problems through physical inspection alone.}
\end{notebox}

\section*{\fbox{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{1.3 Failure Criteria and Factor of Safety}}}
\vspace{-0.5em}
{\small\textit{Ugural and Fenster 4.1--4.11}}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{1.3 Failure Criteria and Factor of Safety}\\
Failure criteria account for the combined 3D state of stress to predict material failure, rather than simply comparing individual stress components against the material strength. The appropriate failure criterion depends on the type of material and its failure mechanism.
\subsection*{Maximum Shear Stress Criterion (Tresca)}
\textbf{Assumptions and intuition}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Isotropic, ductile material with \textit{uniaxial} yield strength \(\sigma_y\)
    \item \hl{Yielding occurs when the absolute maximum shear stress reaches a critical value}.
    \item Conservative criterion for predicting ductile material yielding.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Derivation}\\
Consider a uniaxial tension test:
\[
\sigma_x=\frac{F}{A}=\sigma,\qquad \sigma_y=\sigma_z=0
\]
\[
\tau_{xy}=\tau_{xz}=\tau_{yz}=0
\]
At the onset of yielding, \(\sigma=\sigma_Y\). From Mohr's circle:
\[
\tau_{\max}=\frac{\sigma-0}{2}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\tau_Y=\frac{\sigma_Y}{2}
\]
For a general 3D state of stress with unordered principal stresses
$\sigma_a,\sigma_b,\sigma_c$, the maximum shear stress criterion requires
\[
\boxed{
\tau_{\max}=\frac{1}{2}\max\left(
|\sigma_a-\sigma_b|,
|\sigma_b-\sigma_c|,
|\sigma_c-\sigma_a|
\right)\leq\frac{\sigma_Y}{2}
}
\]
Equivalently, when the principal stresses are ordered such that
$\sigma_1\geq\sigma_2\geq\sigma_3$,
\[
\boxed{\sigma_1-\sigma_3\leq\sigma_Y}
\]
For plane stress, taking $\sigma_c=0$, the criterion reduces to
\[
|\sigma_a-\sigma_b|\leq\sigma_Y,\quad
|\sigma_a|\leq\sigma_Y,\quad
|\sigma_b|\leq\sigma_Y
\]
These three constraints define the hexagonal region in the
$\sigma_a$-$\sigma_b$ plane.\\\\
\noindent
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.55\textwidth}
    \vspace{0pt}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Inside: No yielding.
        \item On the boundary: Onset of yielding.
        \item Outside: Tresca criterion predicts yielding.
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.40\textwidth}
    \vspace{0pt}
    \centering
    \img[1.0]{tresca.png}
\end{minipage}\\
\textbf{Physical interpretation:} Yielding is predicted to begin when the radius of the largest Mohr's circle reaches $\sigma_Y/2$.
\newpage
\subsection*{Maximum Distortion Energy Criterion (von Mises)}
\textbf{Assumptions and intuition}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Isotropic, ductile material with \textit{uniaxial} yield strength \(\sigma_y\)
    \item \hl{Yielding based on the distortional strain energy.}
    \item Generally more accurate than the Tresca criterion for predicting ductile material yielding.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Derivation}\\
From the textbook, the distortional energy per unit volume, expressed in terms of the unordered principal stresses $\sigma_a,\sigma_b,\sigma_c$, is:
\[
U_{od}=\frac{1+\nu}{6E}
\left[(\sigma_a-\sigma_b)^2+(\sigma_b-\sigma_c)^2
+(\sigma_c-\sigma_a)^2\right]
\]
\textit{Note that since we are squaring the differences between all pairs of principal stresses and summing them up, it doesn't matter whether we order the principal stresses or not; the expression is symmetric!}

For a uniaxial tension test at yielding, substitute
$\sigma_a=\sigma_Y$, $\sigma_b=\sigma_c=0$:
\[
U_{od,Y}=\frac{1+\nu}{3E}\sigma_Y^2
\]
Equating $U_{od}=U_{od,Y}$ gives the yield condition:
\[
(\sigma_a-\sigma_b)^2+(\sigma_b-\sigma_c)^2
+(\sigma_c-\sigma_a)^2=2\sigma_Y^2
\]
Define the von Mises effective stress $\sigma_e$:
\[
\boxed{
\sigma_e=\sqrt{\frac{(\sigma_a-\sigma_b)^2
+(\sigma_b-\sigma_c)^2+(\sigma_c-\sigma_a)^2}{2}}
}
\]
The criterion becomes
\[
\boxed{\sigma_e\leq\sigma_Y}
\]
For plane stress, taking $\sigma_c=0$, the effective stress reduces to
\[
\boxed{\sigma_e=\sqrt{\sigma_a^2-\sigma_a\sigma_b+\sigma_b^2}}
\]
\newpage
The von Mises yield criterion defines an elliptical region enclosing the Tresca hexagon, indicating that the Tresca criterion is more conservative.
\img[0.4]{von mises.png}
\subsection*{Mohr's Criterion}
\textbf{Assumptions and intuition}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Isotropic material with \textit{different} uniaxial yield strengths in tension and compression
    \item Failure occurs when the principal stresses fall outside the failure hexagon.
    \item \hl{Conservative criterion for brittle material fracture.}
\end{itemize}
Define:
\[
\sigma_{UT}=\text{Ultimate tensile strength}
\]
\[
\sigma_{UC}=\text{Ultimate compressive strength}
\]
Both are defined as positive magnitudes.


For a general 3D stress state, order the principal stresses such that
$\sigma_1\geq\sigma_2\geq\sigma_3$.

The Mohr criterion can be summarized in three cases:
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabular}{l l}
\hline
\textbf{Stress state} & \textbf{Condition for no fracture} \\
\hline
All tensile & $\sigma_1<\sigma_{UT}$ \\
All compressive & $|\sigma_3|<\sigma_{UC}$ \\
Combined tension and compression &
$\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_{UT}}
-\frac{\sigma_3}{\sigma_{UC}}<1$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\textit{Note that the intermediate principal stress $\sigma_2$ does matter for the criterion.}
\newpage
For plane stress, taking $\sigma_c=0$ and using the unordered principal stresses $\sigma_a$ and $\sigma_b$, the criterion defines a hexagonal failure envelope in the $\sigma_a$-$\sigma_b$ plane:
\img[0.4]{mohrs.png}
\subsection*{Factor of Safety}
Define the allowable equivalent stress:
\[
\boxed{\sigma_{all}=\frac{\sigma_Y}{FS}}
\]
Substitute the allowable stress into the respective failure criterion:
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.8}
\begin{tabular}{l l}
\hline
\textbf{Criterion} & \textbf{Design requirement} \\
\hline
Tresca & $\displaystyle\tau_{\max}\leq\frac{\sigma_Y}{2FS}$ \\
Von Mises & $\displaystyle\sigma_e\leq\frac{\sigma_Y}{FS}$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Alternatively, to determine the factor of safety for a given state of stress:
\[
FS_T=\frac{\sigma_Y}{2\tau_{\max}},\quad FS_{VM}=\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_e}
\]
\textbf{Important Takeaways:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Always account for the 3D state of stress. Even for plane stress, the zero principal stress may be the maximum or minimum principal stress.
    \item Choose the appropriate failure criterion based on the material.
    \item Under combined loading, apply the factor of safety to the material strength $\sigma_Y$ before solving for the allowable load. Do not simply divide the load at yielding by the factor of safety when other loads remain constant.
\end{itemize}

\section*{\fbox{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{1.4 Infinitesimal Strain Tensor and Strain Transformations}}}
\vspace{-0.5em}
{\small\textit{Ugural and Fenster 2.2--2.5}}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{1.4 Infinitesimal Strain Tensor and Strain Transformations}\\
Unlike stress, which describes the distribution of internal forces, strain describes the local deformation of a material.

Much of the mathematics developed for stress transformations can be reused for strain transformations. \textit{The main difference to be careful about is the factor of two associated with engineering shear strain.}
\subsection*{Displacement field and infinitesimal strain}
The displacement field describes the displacement of any point $(x,y,z)$ in a body from its original position to its deformed position:
\[
\vec u(x,y,z)=u(x,y,z)\hat i+v(x,y,z)\hat j+w(x,y,z)\hat k
\]
where $u,v,w$ are the displacement components in the $x,y,z$ directions, respectively. The deformed position of the material point is therefore
\[
(x',y',z')=(x+u,y+v,z+w)
\]
While displacement describes how much a material point moves, strain describes how the displacement varies between points in the material.

\textbf{Normal Strain}\\
Normal strain describes the relative change in length of an infinitesimal material line element.
\[
\varepsilon=\lim_{\Delta L\to0}\frac{\Delta L'-\Delta L}{\Delta L}
\]
Under the infinitesimal deformation assumption:
\[
\boxed{
\varepsilon_x=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x},\qquad
\varepsilon_y=\frac{\partial v}{\partial y},\qquad
\varepsilon_z=\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}
}
\]
Deformation due to normal strain for a 2D element in the \(x\)-\(y\) plane:
\img[0.5]{deformation from normal strain.png}
\textbf{Engineering shear strain}\\
Engineering shear strain \(\gamma_{xy}\) describes the decrease in angle, in radians, between two originally perpendicular lines 
\img[0.5]{engineering shear strain.png}
\textit{The engineering shear strain $\gamma_{xy}$ represents the change in angle from $\angle DAB$ to $\angle D'AB'$, measured in radians.}

From the small angle approximation, the engineering shear strain can be expressed in terms of the displacement field:
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\gamma_{xy}&=\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\\
\gamma_{yz}&=\frac{\partial v}{\partial z}+\frac{\partial w}{\partial y}\\
\gamma_{xz}&=\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}+\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}
\end{aligned}
}
\]
The tensor shear strain is defined as half the engineering shear strain:
\[
\boxed{\varepsilon_{ij}=\frac{\gamma_{ij}}{2}\qquad(i\ne j)}
\]
\hl{This distinction is important when using shear strain in tensor transformations and Mohr's circle.}
\subsection*{Infinitesimal strain tensor}
The strain components can be assembled into a symmetric second-order tensor:
\[
\boxed{
[\varepsilon]=
\begin{bmatrix}
\varepsilon_x&\frac{\gamma_{xy}}{2}&\frac{\gamma_{xz}}{2}\\
\frac{\gamma_{xy}}{2}&\varepsilon_y&\frac{\gamma_{yz}}{2}\\
\frac{\gamma_{xz}}{2}&\frac{\gamma_{yz}}{2}&\varepsilon_z
\end{bmatrix}
}
\]
\newpage
In terms of index notation, the entries of the strain tensor are given by:
\[
\boxed{
\varepsilon_{ij}=\frac12
\left(
\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}
+\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}
\right)
}
\]
where \(i,j=x,y,z\).

\textit{Note that the strain tensor is symmetric by definition, unlike the stress tensor which is symmetric by shear stress symmetry.}
\subsection*{Local rotation vs shear strain}
Define:
\[
\alpha_x\approx\frac{\partial v}{\partial x},
\qquad
\alpha_y\approx-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}
\]
where \(\alpha_x,\alpha_y\) are the rotations of originally \(x\)- and \(y\)-directed faces, respectively, with CCW rotation positive.

The engineering shear strain is the difference between the two rotations:
\[
\boxed{\gamma_{xy}=\alpha_x-\alpha_y
=\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}}
\]
The local net rotation is the average of the two rotations:
\[
\boxed{\omega_z=\frac{\alpha_x+\alpha_y}{2}
=\frac12\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}
-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right)}
\]
\textit{Note that zero strain does not mean zero displacement, the material element can still undergo rigid body translation and rotation without deformation.}
\subsection*{Strain transformations}
Since stress and strain are both second-order tensors, their transformation equations have the same mathematical form. Furthermore, the textbook explicitly states that the stress transformation equations can be converted into strain transformation equations using the substitutions:
\[
\boxed{\sigma\rightarrow\varepsilon,\qquad
\tau\rightarrow\frac{\gamma}{2}}
\]
\textbf{General 3D strain transformation}\\
Using the same direction cosine matrix $[L]$ as for stress transformations, the strain transformation is:
\[
\boxed{[\varepsilon']=[L][\varepsilon][L]^{tr}}
\]
\newpage
\textbf{2D Strain transformations}\\
The approach to 2D strain transformations is the same as for 2D stress transformations. We can use Mohr's circle relations to determine strain transformations in the $x$-$y$ plane:
\img[0.7]{mohrs strain.png}
\textit{Note that the Mohr's circle uses the strain tensor shear $\varepsilon_{xy}$ instead of the engineering shear $\gamma_{xy}$}

Otherwise, the usual Mohr's circle relations apply, let:
\[
\Delta\varepsilon=\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y,\qquad
\varepsilon_{ave}=\frac{\varepsilon_x+\varepsilon_y}{2}
\]
\[
\boxed{
\varepsilon_{x'}=\varepsilon_{ave}
+\frac{\Delta\varepsilon}{2}\cos2\theta
+\frac{\gamma_{xy}}{2}\sin2\theta
}
\]
\[
\boxed{
\varepsilon_{y'}=\varepsilon_{ave}
-\frac{\Delta\varepsilon}{2}\cos2\theta
-\frac{\gamma_{xy}}{2}\sin2\theta
}
\]
\[
\boxed{\varepsilon_{x'y'}=
\frac{\gamma_{x'y'}}{2}
=\frac{\gamma_{xy}}{2}\cos2\theta
-\frac{\Delta\varepsilon}{2}\sin2\theta
}
\]
The principal directions are defined as the directions where the shear strain is zero \(\varepsilon_{x'y'}=0\):
\[
\boxed{\tan2\theta_p=\frac{\gamma_{xy}}{\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y}}
\]
with corresponding principal strains:
\[
\boxed{
\varepsilon_{a,b}=\varepsilon_{ave}\pm R,\quad 
R=\sqrt{
\left(\frac{\Delta\varepsilon}{2}\right)^2+
\left(\frac{\gamma_{xy}}{2}\right)^2
}}
\]

In Part I, we established the mathematical framework for describing the state of stress and strain in a material. In Part II, we will introduce the governing equations of elasticity, which are needed to solve for the unknown stress and strain fields in a deformable body under external loading.

\section*{\fbox{2.1 Equations of Strain Compatibility}}
\vspace{-0.5em}
{\small\textit{Ugural and Fenster 2.4}}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{2.1 Equations of Strain Compatibility}\\
Recall that the stress-displacement relations establish a relationship between six stress components as functions of only three displacement components $u,v,w$. This implies that the six strain components are not independent, and must satisfy certain compatibility conditions to ensure that the resulting strain field is physically possible. 

Therefore, the strain compatibility equations are necessary to ensure that the strain field corresponds to a continuous and single-valued displacement field.

Intuitively, this means that the deformed material must remain continuous such that there aren't any gaps or overlaps in the material. Imagine subdividing a material into infinitesimal elements, the deformation of each element must agree with the deformation of its neighboring elements!
\subsection*{Derivation of the 2D compatibility equation}
Recall the strain-displacement relations from Section 1.4:
\[
\varepsilon_x=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x},\qquad
\varepsilon_y=\frac{\partial v}{\partial y},\qquad
\gamma_{xy}=\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}
\]
Differentiating the normal strain components twice:
\[
\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_x}{\partial y^2}
=\frac{\partial^3u}{\partial x\partial y^2}
\]
\[
\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_y}{\partial x^2}
=\frac{\partial^3v}{\partial x^2\partial y}
\]
Taking the mixed partial derivative of the engineering shear strain:
\[
\frac{\partial^2\gamma_{xy}}{\partial x\partial y}
=\frac{\partial^3u}{\partial x\partial y^2}
+\frac{\partial^3v}{\partial x^2\partial y}
\]
Assuming sufficiently smooth displacement functions such that the order of partial differentiation is interchangeable, we obtain the 2D strain compatibility equation:
\[
\boxed{
\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_x}{\partial y^2}
+\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_y}{\partial x^2}
=\frac{\partial^2\gamma_{xy}}{\partial x\partial y}
}
\]
Alternatively, in terms of tensor shear strain:
\[
\boxed{
\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_x}{\partial y^2}
+\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_y}{\partial x^2}
=2\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_{xy}}{\partial x\partial y}
}
\]
Notice that we have \hl{eliminated the displacement components entirely, obtaining an equation expressed solely in terms of strains.}
\subsection*{3D compatibility equations}
The same procedure can be generalized to three dimensions, resulting in six compatibility equations.
\[
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_x}{\partial y^2}
+\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_y}{\partial x^2}
&=\frac{\partial^2\gamma_{xy}}{\partial x\partial y},
\qquad&
2\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_x}{\partial y\partial z}
&=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}
\left(
-\frac{\partial\gamma_{yz}}{\partial x}
+\frac{\partial\gamma_{xz}}{\partial y}
+\frac{\partial\gamma_{xy}}{\partial z}
\right)
\\[10pt]
\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_y}{\partial z^2}
+\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_z}{\partial y^2}
&=\frac{\partial^2\gamma_{yz}}{\partial y\partial z},
\qquad&
2\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_y}{\partial z\partial x}
&=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}
\left(
\frac{\partial\gamma_{yz}}{\partial x}
-\frac{\partial\gamma_{xz}}{\partial y}
+\frac{\partial\gamma_{xy}}{\partial z}
\right)
\\[10pt]
\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_z}{\partial x^2}
+\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_x}{\partial z^2}
&=\frac{\partial^2\gamma_{xz}}{\partial z\partial x},
\qquad&
2\frac{\partial^2\varepsilon_z}{\partial x\partial y}
&=\frac{\partial}{\partial z}
\left(
\frac{\partial\gamma_{yz}}{\partial x}
+\frac{\partial\gamma_{xz}}{\partial y}
-\frac{\partial\gamma_{xy}}{\partial z}
\right)
\end{aligned}}
\]
\textit{All strain fields must satisfy compatibility. This is automatically satisfied for strains derived from sufficiently smooth displacement fields, but must be explicitly enforced when solving directly for strain or stress fields.}
\section*{\fbox{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{2.2 Constitutive Equations:\\ Generalized Hooke’s Law}}}
\vspace{-0.5em}
{\small\textit{Ugural and Fenster 2.7--2.10}}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{2.2 Constitutive Equations: Generalized Hooke’s Law}\\
Like in MechMat, while strain-displacement relations describe how the material deforms geometrically, constitutive equations describe how the material responds mechanically to the deformation.

\textbf{Assumptions}\\
For the generalized Hooke's law, we assume:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The material is linear elastic: stress and strain are linearly related, deformation is fully recoverable (no yielding or plasticity)
    \item Homogeneous: material properties are uniform throughout the body
    \item Isotropic: material properties are the same in all directions
    \item Small deformations: the infinitesimal strain assumption holds
\end{itemize}
\subsection*{Derivation of the generalized Hooke's law}
Recall from MechMat that for a uniaxial loading in the \(x\)-direction:
\[
\varepsilon_x=\frac{\sigma_x}{E},\qquad
\varepsilon_y=\varepsilon_z=-\nu\frac{\sigma_x}{E}
\]
\textit{Where the modulus of elasticity $E$ describes a lengthening in the direction of loading, and Poisson's ratio $\nu$ describes a shortening in the transverse directions.}

\textbf{Biaxial loading}\\
For biaxial loading in the $x$- and $y$-directions, 
consider the deformation in the $x$-direction as shown 
in the figure below.
\img[0.5]{multiaxial loading.png}
Tensile stress $\sigma_x$ causes an elongation in 
the $x$-direction, while the tensile stress $\sigma_y$ 
causes a contraction in the $x$-direction due to the 
Poisson effect.

By superposition, the net strain in the $x$-direction is
\[
\varepsilon_x=
\underbrace{\frac{\sigma_x}{E}}_{\text{Direct elongation}}
-
\underbrace{\frac{\nu\sigma_y}{E}}_{\text{Poisson contraction}}
\]

Extending this approach to loading in all three directions 
and repeating for each strain component gives generalized 
Hooke's law.

\textbf{Generalized Hooke's law (multiaxial loading)}\\
Accounting for the Poisson effect in all three directions, the generalized Hooke's law is:
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\varepsilon_x&=\frac{1}{E}[\sigma_x-\nu(\sigma_y+\sigma_z)]
&\qquad \gamma_{xy}&=\frac{\tau_{xy}}{G}
\\[6pt]
\varepsilon_y&=\frac{1}{E}[\sigma_y-\nu(\sigma_x+\sigma_z)]
&\qquad \gamma_{yz}&=\frac{\tau_{yz}}{G}
\\[6pt]
\varepsilon_z&=\frac{1}{E}[\sigma_z-\nu(\sigma_x+\sigma_y)]
&\qquad \gamma_{xz}&=\frac{\tau_{xz}}{G}
\end{aligned}
}
\]
Where $E$ is the modulus of elasticity (Young's modulus), $\nu$ is Poisson's ratio, and $G$ is the shear modulus. 

These three properties are related to each other in linear isotropic materials by the equation:
\[
\boxed{G=\frac{E}{2(1+\nu)}}
\]
\textit{Note that due to the Poisson effect, a zero normal stress in a direction does not imply a zero normal strain in that direction. Likewise, zero strain does not imply zero stress.}
